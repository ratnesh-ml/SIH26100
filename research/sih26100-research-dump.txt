Chennai Petroleum Corporation Limited (CPCL) — a Fortune-500 IndianOil Group refinery — issues bids through GeM and its own CPCL e-Procurement portal (cpcletenders.nic.in) using a two-bid system requiring EMD, Integrity Pact, MSE certificates, GST, PAN, Udyam, OEM authorization, ITR, PPP-MII local content declarations, and technical qualification proofs (TenderShark). The CAG Union Commercial Audit Report No. 18 of 2020, Chapter IV, is damning: PAN numbers were unverified in 42.79% of records, CIN numbers missing in 36.12% of company sellers, 12.33% of orders worth ₹792.41 crore were awarded after delays up to 312 days, and 39.64% of orders had delivery issues (CAG Report 2020). The market is not empty — Tender Intel is already deployed live at CPCL for mechanical works tender evaluation (tenderintelai.org). Global suites like SAP Ariba Supplier Risk, Coupa, Icertis, Zycus Merlin, and JAGGAER exist but, as Arched.ai's 10-platform comparison proves, none of them integrate natively with GeM/CPPP portals nor understand Indian regulatory artifacts — Udyam, PPP-MII Class-I/II local content self-declarations under DPIIT PPP-MII Order 2017 (DPIIT), GFR 2017 Rule 144(xi) restrictions on land-border countries (CSIR notification), MSME Order, or Integrity Pact clauses. That is the wedge.

Our proposal — VigilBid: AI-Powered Integrated Bid Compliance Verification Platform — is a Human-in-the-Loop Decision Support System that ingests a bidder ZIP, performs multimodal Document AI extraction (LayoutLMv3 + PaddleOCR-VL for stamps/handwriting achieving 91.66% on OmniDocBench v1.5 PaddleOCR-VL 1.5 paper, arXiv 2601.21957), cross-verifies every artifact against live source-of-truth APIs (DigiLocker Issued Documents API apisetu.gov.in/digilocker, MCA21 V3, GSTN, NSDL PAN, Udyam Aadhaar), runs a Graph Neural Network vendor-collusion detector grounded in Gomes et al. 2024 (arXiv 2410.07091), a PDF Forensics engine based on Group-IB's incremental-update analysis (Group-IB PDF Tampering deep-dive) and Sudheer et al.'s file-page-object technique (arXiv 2507.00827), and a RAG Compliance Copilot grounded in Sun, Luo & Li's COLING 2025 framework (ACL Anthology) that generates evidence-backed, page-cited justifications tied to the exact clause of GFR 2017 or CVC circular. Every AI verdict comes with a confidence band and page-level provenance — the officer decides, the machine documents.

Ten differentiators separate VigilBid from Tender Intel and all incumbents: (1) Live source-of-truth API cross-verification (Tender Intel only reads what's in the ZIP), (2) Vendor Trust Graph built from MCA21 director/CIN edges, (3) PDF Metadata + Incremental-Update Tamper Detection, (4) Fuzzy PPP-MII Local Content Auditor, (5) Explainable Risk Heatmap, (6) RAG-grounded Officer Copilot with GFR/CVC citations, (7) Signature Verification via Siamese CNN (SigNet, Dey et al., 2017), (8) Blockchain-anchored Immutable Audit Trail satisfying CVC RTI, (9) Bid-Rigging Red-Flag Radar using OECD/IACRC 20-scheme taxonomy (IACRC), and (10) Air-Gapped On-Premise Deployment with PII redaction for petroleum-sector data sovereignty. Target metrics: ≥ 92% OCR accuracy on Indian stamped/handwritten forms, ≥ 88% entity-match F1, 8-hour → 12-minute average verification cycle, 65% officer time saved.

📌 SECTION 1 — PROBLEM DECOMPOSITION
1.1 Existing Workflow (Step-by-Step at CPCL & Analogous PSUs)
Chennai Petroleum Corporation Limited follows a two-bid (Techno-Commercial + Priced) e-procurement workflow governed by GFR 2017 Rule 144, CVC guidelines, and CPCL's Delegation of Purchase Authority. The step-by-step process, reconstructed from the actual CPCL tender document CPCLS25471 (Scribd copy) and CPCL's GeM bid document 7451270 hosted at bidplus.gem.gov.in, is as follows.

Stage 1 — Publication: NIT (Notice Inviting Tender) uploaded to cpcletenders.nic.in and, for common-use goods, to GeM under the two-bid workflow described in the GeM Technical Bid Evaluation SOP. Stage 2 — Bidder Submission: bidder uploads a ZIP containing EMD proof (or exemption via MSE certificate), signed Integrity Pact, GST Registration Certificate, PAN, Udyam/MSME Certificate, last-3-years financial statements + ITR, OEM Authorization Letter per GeM's Annexure-I format (GeM fulfilment portal), PPP-MII Local Content Declaration under DPIIT Order 2017, PF/ESIC registrations, technical compliance sheet, and past-performance proofs. Stage 3 — Bid Opening & Preliminary Screening: procurement officers download every bidder ZIP, unpack into folders, and begin a manual criterion-by-criterion inspection. Stage 4 — Cross-Portal Verification: officers hop between GSTN portal, MCA21 for CIN, Udyam Registration Portal, DigiLocker if referenced, PFMS for financial checks, CVC's debarment list, and the Ministry of Finance's Rule 144(xi) list of restricted countries. Stage 5 — Compliance Matrix Population: officers hand-fill Excel scorecards mapping each bid to each eligibility clause. Stage 6 — TEC (Technical Evaluation Committee) meeting: physical or virtual review, dissenting notes recorded. Stage 7 — Price Bid Opening for qualified bidders. Stage 8 — LOA (Letter of Award) issuance. Stage 9 — Audit trail retention for CAG/CVC and RTI requests.

Steps 3–5 alone consume the bulk of officer time. The Arched.ai comparison of Indian e-procurement platforms notes that no corporate procurement suite (SAP Ariba, Coupa, GEP SMART, Zycus, Ivalua, Kissflow, C1 India) provides government-tender document parsing — the officer, therefore, does this by hand.

1.2 Pain Points — Quantified
The Comptroller and Auditor General of India's audit of the GeM platform between January 2018 and March 2019 is the single most damning primary source we have. Its numeric findings, in ₹16,286.99 crore of GeM procurement across 13.81 lakh orders, are the empirical spine of this problem statement (CAG Union Commercial Report No. 18 of 2020, Chapter IV). PAN numbers were unverified by the system in 42.79% of registered seller records; CIN numbers were not fed into the system in 36.12% of company-type sellers; 12.33% of orders valuing ₹792.41 crore were awarded after delays ranging from five days up to 312 days; 39.64% of orders had delivery either not initiated or late; and payments occurred within time in only 3.79% of CRACs. In effect, four-out-of-ten sellers on the country's flagship procurement platform have unverified identity documents.

Grassroots evidence complements the audit. A LinkedIn post from procurement consultant Ram Kumar (October 2026) documents that 67% of MSMEs lost government tenders in a single month due to MSE verification delays and 82% of rejected bids had only minor documentation gaps (LinkedIn Oct 2026; related MSE post). GST-side fraud is nationally systemic: The Hindu reported detection of ₹1,200 crore of fake bills routed through 20 fake companies from a single computer (The Hindu, Karnataka); Indian Express detailed how stolen identities were used to fake GST registrations in Noida (Indian Express); Times of India reported a Gurgaon racket that used a job portal to create shell firms (TOI); and CBIC officers detected ₹15,851 crore of fraudulent ITC claims in April–June 2024 across 3,558 fake firms. This is the raw material that flows into government procurement.

The pain crystallises into six measurable dimensions. Time: officers spend an estimated 6–10 person-hours per bidder on multi-portal cross-checks in a typical CPCL-scale tender with 15–40 bidders — approximately 100–400 hours per tender. Errors: manual data-entry error rates in Indian PSUs are documented at 4–7% (CVC circulars on preventive vigilance). Fraud: fictitious-vendor and false-statement schemes are recognized by IACRC's 20-scheme taxonomy as chronic. Auditability: 51% of transactions in the CAG-audited period lacked full traceability trails. Compliance: even the debarment check requires officers to manually inspect the CPPP Debarment List plus the World Bank ineligible-firms list (worldbank.org). Risk of Litigation: the S. Haja Najeemudden v. CVC judgment (Indian Kanoon) sets a precedent that prima facie forgery in tender documents demands rigorous documentary proof for disciplinary action — meaning weak audit trails collapse under review.

🏛️ SECTION 4 — GOVERNMENT RESEARCH
4.1 Regulatory Framework — Statutes & Circulars VigilBid Must Encode
The Indian public procurement stack that CPCL operates under is a layered instrument. At the base sits the General Financial Rules 2017, promulgated by the Department of Expenditure, whose Rule 144 makes GeM the default procurement platform for common-use goods and services (Scribd copy of GFR Rules 143–207; CSSMitra explainer; TenderKart summary). Rule 144(xi), inserted in July 2020, imposes the land-border-country restriction — bidders from countries sharing a land border with India (China, Pakistan and others) require registration with the DPIIT-designated competent authority (CSIR notification on Rule 144(xi); Chambers global guide). Rule 151 governs blacklisting and debarment; the Department of Expenditure debarment guidelines, the CPPP Debarment List, and the World Bank ineligible firms list form the multi-source debarment universe VigilBid must query.

The Public Procurement (Preference to Make in India) Order 2017, notified by DPIIT under Rule 153(iii) of GFR (DPIIT official), defines Class-I Local Supplier (≥50% local content) and Class-II Local Supplier (≥20%) with amendments through July 2024 (TenderFlow Pro 2024–26 guide; TenderKart PPP-MII explainer; Lexology piece by senior counsel). Class-I gets purchase preference and can win even at a 20% price premium in "only Class-I" tenders; Class-II gets a lower tier. Both classes must submit a self-declaration of local-content percentage, and above the ₹10 crore threshold this must be certified by a statutory/cost auditor. Nodal ministries fix the local-content thresholds product-by-product.

CPCL and its parent Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas layer their own procurement policy on top — the CPCL tender document CPCLS25471 explicitly notes "CPCL reserves the right to obtain additional documents for verifying eligibility of MSE bidder" and requires signing of an Integrity Pact (TenderShark CPCL guide; CPCL bid document 7088117). The Integrity Pact is a Transparency-International-derived instrument monitored by Independent External Monitors (IEMs) — see the NTPC IP page, the TCIL Integrity Pact page, and the MEA IP resource — and must be signed by the bidder's authorized signatory.

CVC oversight cuts across everything. The Central Vigilance Commission's inquiry orders (cvc.gov.in/inquirymatters) and the guidelines aggregated in the Bid India explainer (bidindia.co.in) form a body all central entities must respect. The famous S. Haja Najeemudden v. CVC case (Indian Kanoon) — where the Court found prima facie evidence of "forgery, impersonation, and falsification of documents" in tender submissions — is the judicial anchor that mandates rigorous documentary evidence trails. ⚙️ SECTION 9 — TECH STACK RESEARCH
The tech-stack decisions below have been anchored to head-to-head benchmarks and open-source community activity, not opinion. FastAPI vs Django: FastAPI wins on async I/O throughput (crucial when we fan out six verification adapters in parallel), on typed request/response models via Pydantic (essential when marshaling verification-event schemas across services), on lower memory footprint per worker (on-prem VMs at CPCL will not be lavishly sized), and on OpenAPI generation for the CPCL SAP-ERP integration team; Django wins only if you need a monolithic admin CMS, which we do not. Postgres vs MongoDB: Postgres wins because our data is highly relational (bidder ↔ tender ↔ criterion ↔ verification-event), because JSONB handles the flexible extraction payload natively, and because the pgvector extension gives us vector search for RAG without introducing a second DB engine; Mongo would proliferate query languages and cost us joins on the audit trail. LayoutLMv3 vs Donut: LayoutLMv3 wins on structured KV extraction accuracy (0.88 vs 0.74 in the Bajrami et al. comparative analysis published on ResearchGate) — but we retain Donut as a fallback for OCR-quality-degraded scans where its OCR-free encoder shines (Kim et al. 2022, arXiv 2111.15664). PaddleOCR vs Tesseract: PaddleOCR-VL 1.5 reaches 91.66% on OmniDocBench v1.5 (arXiv 2601.21957) and supports Hindi/Tamil/Devanagari-Latin mixed docs; Tesseract, while venerable, is empirically weaker on Indian scripts and complex scenes per Toon Beerten's OCR comparison and the Codesota Python-OCR-library benchmark (codesota.com). LangChain vs Haystack: Haystack is pipeline-first and typically 40–50% faster generator latency (Haystack ~5.9 ms vs LangChain ~10 ms per AIMultiple benchmark) and shines on document retrieval, while LangChain is agent-first and shines on multi-tool reasoning; because our Compliance Copilot uses multi-agent clause decomposition per Das et al. ACM TOSEM 2026, we choose LangChain with LangGraph for the agent orchestration but embed Haystack-style retrieval pipelines under the hood for latency.

💎 SECTION 10 — 10 KILLER DIFFERENTIATORS
#1 Live Source-of-Truth Cross-Verification Engine. Every certificate uploaded is verified against the actual issuing authority in near-real-time via DigiLocker Issued Documents API, MCA21 V3, GSTN, NSDL PAN, and Udyam Aadhaar. Tender Intel, per its own page, "reads your documents" — it doesn't call GSTN back. Why judges love it: solves the CAG's 42.79%-unverified-PAN finding directly. Difficulty: medium (adapter development). Demo impact: high — live "fake GST" vs "real GST" side-by-side in the demo.

#2 Vendor Trust Graph with GNN Collusion Scoring. Splink resolves bidder entities; a heterogeneous graph is built with bidder / director / address / phone-number / bank-account nodes and MCA21-derived edges; a GraphSAGE GNN scores collusion risk per bidder pair, echoing the technique validated by Gomes et al., arXiv 2410.07091 and the Springer 133-citation review (Lyra et al.). Why judges love it: turns the abstract "shell company scam" news headlines (TOI Gurgaon shell-firm racket, The Hindu ₹1,200 cr fake billing) into an operational signal. Difficulty: high. Demo impact: very high — visualizing a red hyper-edge between two "unrelated" bidders is a jaw-drop moment.

#3 PDF Metadata + Incremental-Update Tamper Detector. Uses PyMuPDF to inspect PDF metadata (Producer, ModDate vs CreationDate deltas, incremental-update generations), catching post-signing edits per Group-IB's field guide, Sudheer et al. arXiv 2507.00827, and the TrueDoc metadata forensics guide. Why judges love it: no incumbent does this at bidder level. Difficulty: medium. Demo impact: high — flash a tampered vs untampered demo PDF.

#4 RAG Compliance Copilot with GFR/CVC/PPP-MII Citations. LangChain + FAISS over a curated KB of GFR 2017 (esp. Rule 144 and Rule 151), CVC circulars, PPP-MII Order 2017 amendments through July 2024, Rule 144(xi) land-border restriction (CSIR notification); every AI verdict cites the exact clause. Operationalises Sun, Luo & Li COLING 2025. Why judges love it: builds officer trust; audit-ready. Difficulty: medium. Demo impact: very high — officer asks "why did you flag this?" and the copilot answers "Rule 153(iii) DPIIT Order 2017, Class-I local content self-declaration missing statutory-auditor certification for tender value ₹18 cr > ₹10 cr threshold".

#5 Fuzzy PPP-MII Local Content Auditor. Reads Class-I / Class-II self-declarations, extracts % local content, cross-checks against the DPIIT PPP-MII order thresholds and per-product notifications; flags declarations lacking statutory-auditor certification above ₹10 cr; flags inconsistent local-content claims across a bidder's history. Why judges love it: shows domain depth beyond generic OCR. Difficulty: medium. Demo impact: high — a "Made in India" declaration flagged for a Rule 144(xi) parent-country conflict is dramatic.

#6 SigNet-Powered Signature Verifier. Siamese CNN verifies authorized-signatory signatures on OEM Annexure-I letters and Integrity Pacts against an internal registry, using Dey et al.'s SigNet architecture and few-shot extensions (Majumder et al. 2023). Why judges love it: closes the fake-OEM-letter loophole. Difficulty: medium-high. Demo impact: high.

#7 Explainable Risk Heatmap. Per-bidder × per-criterion R/A/G tile-view; hover reveals confidence band, evidence page, and the RAG-cited rule; drives officer trust — validated by the XAI-in-regulated-industries literature (Coussement 2024, DSS). Why: converts opaque model into decision cockpit. Difficulty: low. Demo impact: high.

#8 Blockchain-Anchored Immutable Audit Trail. Every officer action + AI verdict is hash-chained (Merkle-tree style); daily root committed to a permissioned ledger (Hyperledger Fabric); satisfies the Haja Najeemudden v. CVC evidentiary standard (Indian Kanoon) and RTI. Why: differentiator for CVC/CAG. Difficulty: medium. Demo impact: medium (but a killer bullet in the pitch).

#9 Phantom-Bidder Detector. Implements Ganguly's SSRN 2026 finding (Phantom Third Bidder paper) — flags patterns where the minimum-bidder-count rule is being gamed by phantom bidders never intended to win. Cross-references CPPP historical data. Why: uniquely Indian; no global incumbent has this. Difficulty: high. Demo impact: very high — first product to name this pattern.

#10 Air-Gapped On-Prem Deployment with PII Redaction. Petroleum PSUs cannot use cloud LLMs (Groq-hosted approaches like the tenderSortV2 repo fail this bar). Our stack — Llama-3.1-8B on-prem via vLLM, MinIO, Postgres, all containerised — deploys inside CPCL's air-gapped VLAN. Why: critical acceptance criterion at CPCL. Difficulty: medium. Demo impact: makes the sale.

D. Data Limitations Disclosure
Certain figures cited above (e.g., 82% rejected-bid rate for minor documentation gaps, 67% MSME loss rate to MSE verification delays) originate from a procurement practitioner's LinkedIn posts (Ram Kumar, Oct 2026 and related MSE post) rather than a peer-reviewed audit. They are directionally consistent with the CAG-audited findings (CAG Report No. 18 of 2020) but should be validated against a wider empirical study during Phase-2. Officer-timing estimates (6–10 person-hours per bidder) are practitioner-derived and should be baselined during CPCL pilot deployment.
